01

The algorithm changed. Our approach has to change with it.

Before we get into what we're building, it's worth being precise about why. Meta's ad system doesn't work the way it did even eighteen months ago, and a lot of "creative best practice" hasn't caught up.

Recent analysis of Meta's ad-serving stack lays out the shift clearly. Meta now runs on three layers working together: GEM, the prediction engine that ingests trillions of signals to forecast which ad produces which action, in near-real-time; Lattice, which consolidates every ad ever run into one searchable library so Meta can compare performance across objectives; and Andromeda, which acts as a personal ads concierge that filters billions of ads down to the handful that fit an individual's taste and behavior.

The "same ad" problem

Here's the part that should change how we plan creative: Andromeda doesn't judge an ad as new or different based on the copy, the offer, or the CTA. It judges "sameness" almost entirely on the visual: for a static or carousel, the aesthetic; for a video, the first three seconds. The practical rule is blunt: different copy or a different call-to-action on the same visual still reads as the same ad.

In practice: the same creator, in the same setting, with three different headlines, reads to Meta as one ad wearing three outfits, not three ads. Swapping a CTA button color or testing new ad copy on an old visual isn't real creative testing anymore. It's the same input, and Meta will hand back the same result.

That's also the likely explanation for something we've already seen in this account: the original Comfort Studs ad held a healthy, stable frequency and still lost half its click-through rate by week 15. It wasn't overexposed; it just stopped being visually new to the system, long before it stopped being new to us.

What actually counts as different

The fix isn't more ads; it's fewer, more deliberately distinct ones. The recommendation is to spend roughly 80% of creative effort on the hook: the first three seconds of a video, or the entire first impression of a static, because that's the window Andromeda weighs most heavily when it decides whether something is worth serving to someone new. Everything after that hook has some flexibility. The hook has almost none.

This broader framework replaces "make more variants of the winner" with what's called a Structural Iteration Matrix: breaking an ad into its real components (who it's for, the concept, the hook, the setting, the type of proof, the brand cues) and deliberately recombining them, rather than nudging the same combination slightly. Tied to that is a "Worlds" framework: building a small number of repeatable, distinct visual environments (a setting, a lighting style, a cast, a palette) the way a sitcom reuses its apartment set. Different personas get different worlds. The same face, in the same room, is not a new world no matter what the caption says.

Five human drives

Underneath the algorithmic mechanics, the argument is almost old-fashioned: winning creative still has to hit a real, timeless motivator. Five stand out.

Survival

Fear turned into agency: a clear problem and a clear way out.

Admiration

Status signaled through belonging, not through boasting.

Success

Visible, measurable transformation.

Love

Authentic connection and belonging.

Protection

Conviction in defending what your tribe values.

The conclusion is the one we're building this plan around: the era of driving performance by uploading small step-changes to a winning ad is over. Genuine visual diversity, built around a real persona and a real human motivator, is what the system rewards now, and not coincidentally, it's also just better advertising.

What we know about video vs. static on Meta

Two things are true at once here: video has real, structural advantages on this platform, and the reason it wins tracks exactly with the hook-first logic above, not a generic "video is more engaging" argument.

Being straight about this data: the middle and right-hand figures are industry-wide benchmarks aggregated across many advertisers, not a single controlled study. Treat them as a directional case for testing more video, not a promise of what Jane Jewels will see. Once we have your account's own video-vs-static breakdown, we'll replace the benchmark with your actual numbers.

The more useful way to read the video advantage isn't "video is more engaging" in the abstract. It's that video gives Andromeda more surface area to judge a hook on. A static image is one frame; a video's first three seconds can move, reveal, and build, which is exactly the window the algorithm weighs most. That's the throughline from Section 01 to everything in Section 03: we're not choosing video because video is trendy, we're choosing it because it gives the hook more to work with in the window that actually decides distribution.

03

The ideas

Three concepts, each doing a different job. Starting with the one furthest along.

Idea 01: Comfort Studs

"Not Afraid of Your Jewelry"

Women buy real luxury (jewelry, handbags, watches) and then don't wear it, because they're afraid of losing it or damaging it. It sits in a drawer being beautiful for no one. Comfort Studs remove that fear entirely: they look and feel like the real thing, at a price where losing one earring is an inconvenience, not a tragedy.

This builds directly on the yoga-studio direction from our last working session (same product, same emotional territory) with a specific opening and a voiceover built for it. The setting itself should still read as elevated, not budget: either a genuinely luxurious city studio, or something more escapist, like a lush, green Bali-style retreat. Either way, this cannot look like a cheap, stuffy room.

0–2s
Wide shot. A woman seated, legs crossed, eyes closed, wrists resting naturally on her knees. Still, unhurried.
2s
Cut to profile: shoulder to crown, ear centered in frame. This is the sparkle beat: the stud catches light exactly as the cut lands.
2–15s
Hold on the profile. Voiceover begins with the cut and carries the rest of the runtime.

Voiceover

Chic, low, declarative: closer to spoken word than a soft affirmation-app read. Short lines, real silence between them.

"I don't perform for anyone. I adorn myself."

"This isn't for special occasions. Every day is the occasion."

"I am not delicate. I am deliberate."

"Comfort isn't the absence of luxury. It's the mastery of it."

The line

Not Afraid of Your Jewelry.

Vocal reference

For tone and delivery only, not a script source. Naomi Campbell's spoken-word verse below is the register we're writing toward: unhurried, a little imperious, entirely unbothered.

Why this earns its place: it sits squarely in the brand-storyline half of the two-bucket model from our last session (pure feeling, no feature list) while the ear-centered close-up gives it one real value-differentiator beat without breaking the calm of the shot. Runtime is 15 seconds by design: this is a brand-affinity asset, not a short-hook prospecting ad, and it isn't held to the same completion-rate math as those.
Idea 02: Day in Her WorldAI-Generated

"The World's Best Everyday Jewelry"

The reference for this one is the Chanel film you screened for the team, the one built around Margot Robbie moving through Paris in a single continuous, high-production sequence. What reads as effortless there is actually the same actress shot many times over and composited into one frame, doing three things at once. That level of production isn't in budget here, so this is a paired-down version built with editing craft instead of visual effects: the same energy as a "Devil Wears Prada" opening montage, one woman moving through her day, with sharp match cuts standing in for the composite work. Every transition resolves on her ear or her face, so the studs stay the visual anchor even as the scene changes around them.

Beat 1
She hails a cab on the street. Quick cut to her ducking her head into the backseat: the close-up lands here, ear catching the light as she lowers in.
Beat 2
She pushes her sunglasses up from the bridge of her nose to the top of her head. The motion naturally brushes her hair back and reveals the earring in the same gesture.
Beat 3
Open for a third beat once we lock the shot list: a call answered with a hand tucking hair behind the ear is one option, kept in the same "on the move" register as the first two.

One subject, one continuous day, cut together to feel faster and more expensive than it costs to make.

The line

The world's best everyday jewelry™

Why this earns its place: it's the concept the team reacted to most strongly when we screened it live, now scoped to what's actually buildable. It leans hardest into the Admiration driver from Section 01: status signaled through belonging, not through boasting. It also uses Jane's own trademarked tagline as the payoff line, which only works if the film itself earns "the world's best" in how it looks.

Idea 03: Us vs ThemAI-Generated

The price-point static

Two pairs of studs, staged together against a backdrop built to feel expensive: deep blue velvet or suede, in Jane's signature navy, with the shadow of palm fronds falling across the frame for something more resort-editorial than product-shot. One side reads as a traditional, expensive pair from a well-known diamond retailer. The other is the Comfort Stud. We never name the other brand; the comparison is left entirely to the viewer's own assumption, which is what makes it work as "us vs. them" without ever becoming a callout.

The point isn't a price fight. It's letting the viewer do the math themselves once they realize how close the two look.

Why this earns its place: it's the one idea here built entirely around the surprise-value tension from Jane's own positioning: look expensive first, let the price be the relief, never the pitch. It also gives the account a comparison-style static to test, which nothing else in this deck currently covers.
Idea 04: Water SeriesAI-Generated

Statics built around water

A second static series, this time leaning into Jane's blue rather than away from it: water as the motif (submerged stones, droplets, rippling reflections) rather than fabric and shadow. Still image-only, still AI-generated, still meant as the lower-cost counterpart to the video ideas above.
